\begin{abstract}
Neural surrogates for parametric finite-element analyses promise orders-of-
magnitude speedups for design-space exploration, but a raw point prediction
is an incomplete replacement for a trusted FEA solver: the engineer
consuming the prediction needs to know when to trust it and when to defer
back to the reference solution. We build a deployment-calibrated
uncertainty layer for a canonical thin shelf-bracket sizing problem --- a
flat L-shaped corner bracket under a static shelf load, modelled in 2D
plane-stress linear elasticity --- by stacking three ingredients on top of
a MeshGraphNets-style graph neural-network surrogate: a 5-member Deep
Ensemble as the base predictor, a Gaussian quantile rule driven by
ensemble disagreement as the raw interval, and split-conformalized
quantile regression on a held-out validation set as the calibration
wrapper. On a 500-sample in-distribution test set the ensemble attains
1.80\% per-node and 0.42\% peak-stress MAPE, the conformal layer achieves
the Romano 2019 Theorem 1 coverage guarantee empirically at every tested
nominal level (80--95\%), and the interval width remains informative
(Pearson 0.944 sample-level width-vs-error). On a 250-sample
pre-registered out-of-distribution set covering $\pm 20\%$ extrapolation
in each of the three design parameters and corner combinations, the
surrogate's accuracy degrades ($3.10\%$ per-node, $7.26\%$ peak MAPE),
empirical coverage drops below nominal (83.7\% versus 90\%), and the
per-sample ensemble std more than doubles --- the reliability layer
correctly signals the degradation it cannot fully absorb. We convert the
std signal into a defer-to-FEA decision rule (threshold at the 95th
percentile of the ID std distribution) achieving 5\% false-alarm rate on
ID inputs and 58\% detection rate on the hardest OOD condition (corner
extrapolation). The combined system is the paper's deliverable: a
calibrated GNN surrogate that is honest about its own domain.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Finite-element analysis (FEA) is the standard tool for stress prediction on
structural parts, but its cost per evaluation ($\sim 10$~s on modest 2D
meshes, minutes to hours on production 3D meshes) rules it out of
tight-inner-loop design workflows: parameter sweeps, multi-objective
optimization, topology exploration, and interactive CAD~\cite{maurizi2022gnn,
pfaff2021meshgraphnets}. Neural surrogates learn a direct map from the
design parameters (and/or the mesh) to the stress field, reducing
per-evaluation cost to milliseconds on a consumer GPU. Graph neural
networks (GNNs) are a particularly natural fit because they operate on the
native FEA mesh without projecting onto a regular
grid~\cite{pfaff2021meshgraphnets, maurizi2022gnn, gladstone2024gnn}.

\textbf{The problem with a bare surrogate.} A neural surrogate returns a
prediction for every query in the design space, including queries for which
it has no training evidence. An engineer consuming the surrogate cannot
distinguish a trusted prediction from a confident hallucination without
re-running the reference FEA solver --- the very cost the surrogate was
supposed to avoid. The missing ingredient is a calibrated
uncertainty layer: a per-prediction interval whose coverage is documented
and whose width is informative, so that the engineer can apply a simple
decision rule (trust the surrogate when the interval is tight, defer to
FEA when it is wide).

\textbf{Study part.} We study a flat L-shaped corner bracket (hereafter
referred to as the L-bracket), a canonical thin shelf-bracket geometry
with three stress concentrators: an inside fillet at the concave corner
and two through-holes through the flanges (Figure~\ref{fig:bracket_photo}).
The part is modelled in 2D plane-stress linear elasticity under a static
shelf load, with three free design parameters --- the inside fillet radius
$R$, the horizontal-flange hole position $p$, and the flange width $W$
(Figure~\ref{fig:geom}). This geometry is small enough that the full
parametric sweep remains tractable with a local FEA pipeline, but rich
enough to exhibit the tail-heavy stress distribution and the multiple
stress-concentrator locality that characterize real shelf-bracket sizing
problems.

\textbf{Contribution.} The contribution of this paper is the reliability
layer, not the surrogate. We combine three well-established ingredients ---
a MeshGraphNets-style GNN surrogate~\cite{pfaff2021meshgraphnets,
maurizi2022gnn}, a Deep Ensemble for epistemic
uncertainty~\cite{lakshminarayanan2017ensembles}, and split-conformal
quantile regression for calibrated prediction
intervals~\cite{romano2019cqr, angelopoulos2023conformal,
gopakumar2024conformal} --- into a single deployment-calibrated surrogate
for parametric L-bracket stress prediction. We verify the conformal
coverage guarantee empirically at four nominal levels on a held-out ID
test set, show via a pre-registered OOD protocol (fixed before any model
training) that accuracy degrades and coverage drops on geometries outside
the training box while ensemble disagreement more than doubles, and convert
the ensemble-std signal into a concrete defer-to-FEA decision rule with
quantified false-alarm and detection rates.

\section{Related Work}

Graph neural networks are a natural fit for stress-field prediction on
unstructured FEA meshes because they act directly on the native
discretization without projecting onto a regular grid. Maurizi et
al.~\cite{maurizi2022gnn} demonstrate that mesh-based GNNs predict
displacement and stress fields on 2D solid-mechanics benchmarks to within a
few percent of the reference FEA solution. Pfaff et al.'s
MeshGraphNets~\cite{pfaff2021meshgraphnets} supply the
encoder--processor--decoder template with learned edge features that the
present work adopts (details in Methods~\S\,GNN architecture, Phase~1).

Uncertainty quantification for neural surrogates is commonly split into
Bayesian posterior approximations (Monte Carlo dropout, variational BNNs,
deep ensembles) and post-hoc calibration layers on top of a trained point
predictor~\cite{psaros2023uq}. Deep
Ensembles~\cite{lakshminarayanan2017ensembles} are the dominant practical
choice for stress-field surrogates because they reuse the existing
training pipeline and produce a usable epistemic-uncertainty scalar with
no Bayesian machinery, but the raw across-member standard deviation carries
no formal coverage guarantee: it is only a correlate of error. Conformal
prediction~\cite{angelopoulos2023conformal} converts any point or interval
predictor into a prediction interval with finite-sample, distribution-free
coverage, trading the asymptotic guarantees of Bayesian calibration for a
single exchangeability assumption on the calibration set. Romano et
al.~\cite{romano2019cqr} introduce conformalized quantile regression
(CQR), which sharpens the vanilla split-conformal interval to adapt its
width to local heteroscedasticity; the method wraps \emph{any} base
quantile predictor (including ensemble-derived Gaussian quantiles) in a
single additive conformal correction. Gopakumar et
al.~\cite{gopakumar2024conformal} apply conformal prediction cell-wise to
high-dimensional PDE surrogates, pooling nonconformity scores across
spatial cells to obtain marginal per-cell coverage. The present work
combines these three ingredients --- deep ensembles as the base predictor,
CQR as the calibration wrapper, and cell-wise pooling for node-level
marginal coverage on an FEA mesh.

\section{Methods}

\subsection{Problem formulation}

We study an equal-arm symmetric L-bracket under a static shelf load, solved
as a two-dimensional plane-stress linear-elasticity problem. Let
$\Omega \subset \mathbb{R}^2$ denote the bracket cross-section in the $xy$
plane. With out-of-plane thickness $t = 8$~mm and in-plane extent
$A = B = 80$~mm, the aspect ratio $t/A = 1/10$ places the part in the
thin-plate regime, so plane stress (rather than plane strain or full 3D) is
the appropriate reduction. The governing equations are
\begin{equation}
  \nabla \cdot \boldsymbol{\sigma} = \boldsymbol{0}
  \quad \text{in } \Omega,
  \qquad
  \boldsymbol{\sigma} = \mathsf{C}:\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{u}),
  \qquad
  \boldsymbol{\varepsilon} = \tfrac{1}{2}
    \bigl( \nabla \boldsymbol{u} + (\nabla \boldsymbol{u})^\top \bigr),
\end{equation}
with $\mathsf{C}$ the plane-stress isotropic-elasticity tensor and
$\boldsymbol{u}$ the unknown displacement field. Body forces are omitted:
under the calibrated applied load the self-weight contribution to peak
stress is $\sim 10^{-3}$ of the load-driven contribution, below the
discretization error introduced by mesh refinement.

\subsection{Geometry parameterization}

    Three variable parameters: inside fillet radius $R$, Hole-2 position
    along the horizontal flange $p$, and flange width $W$. The back face
    of the vertical flange is clamped; a distributed traction $w$ acts
    downward on the top face of the horizontal flange.}
  \label{fig:geom}
\end{figure}

Three dimensions are exposed as free design parameters:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item $R$ — inside fillet radius at the concave corner.
  \item $p$ — $x$-position of Hole~2 along the centerline of the horizontal
    flange.
  \item $W$ — in-plane flange width, common to both arms.
\end{enumerate}
Parameter ranges were fixed by engineering inspection of limiting
geometries (too-sharp fillet, too-narrow flange, Hole~2 encroaching on the
fillet or the free tip):
\begin{equation}
  R \in [3.0, 10.0]~\text{mm},
  \quad
  p \in [42.0, 72.0]~\text{mm},
  \quad
  W \in [14.0, 24.0]~\text{mm}.
\end{equation}
All remaining dimensions are fixed by design intent. Hole~1 is centered on
the vertical flange at $y = 40$~mm from the top, providing a mounting
point whose location is invariant under the design sweep; Hole~2 has the
same bolt-clearance diameter $D = 8$~mm as Hole~1. The outside corner is
kept sharp: being on the compressive outer bend, it is not a
stress-concentrating feature and adding a second fillet parameter would
dilute the sensitivity analysis without mechanical justification. The
inside-fillet constraint $W + R \leq 34$~mm
(from $y_{\text{Hole 1}} - r_h - \text{clearance} \geq W + R$ with
$y_{\text{Hole 1}} = 40$~mm, $r_h = 4$~mm, clearance $= 2$~mm) binds the
joint upper corner of the sweep box and gives the LHS design a small
rejection rate that is absorbed by oversampling (Section~\ref{ssec:lhs}).

\subsection{Material model}

The bracket is modelled as a single homogeneous linear-elastic isotropic
material. AISI~304 annealed stainless steel is chosen as representative of
a thin shelf-bracket specification. Young's modulus and Poisson's ratio are
taken from the ASM Handbook~\cite{asm_handbook_vol1}:
$E = 193$~GPa, $\nu = 0.29$. The minimum specified yield strength
$\sigma_y = 205$~MPa is the ASTM A240 flat-product value for this
alloy~\cite{astm_a240}. These three constants fully determine the
plane-stress elasticity tensor. Plastic response is not modelled: the
load is calibrated so that the worst-case peak von~Mises stress reaches
$\sim 50\%$ of $\sigma_y$, keeping the full parametric sweep safely in the
elastic regime.

\subsection{Boundary conditions and loading}
\label{ssec:bcs}

A homogeneous Dirichlet condition $\boldsymbol{u} = \boldsymbol{0}$ is
applied on the back face of the vertical flange (the region $x = 0$,
$0 \leq y \leq A$). This idealizes a bracket rigidly mounted to a stiff
planar support; bolt-hole through-thickness contact mechanics are
deliberately omitted and Hole~1, although present as a geometric feature,
carries a traction-free boundary condition rather than a discrete
bolt-reaction force. This reduction is standard for screening-level
sizing analyses and localizes the modelling effort on the geometric
stress concentrators ($R$, holes) rather than on the fastener detail.

The loading models a shelf resting on the top face of the horizontal
flange. A constant downward traction
$\boldsymbol{t} = (0, -w)$~MPa acts on the segment
$W + R \leq x \leq B,\; y = W$. All remaining boundary segments (free tip,
horizontal-flange bottom, vertical-flange top, fillet arc, and both hole
perimeters) are traction-free.

The load magnitude $w$ is calibrated in a single preliminary run on the
worst-case sample $(R, p, W) = (3.0, 42.0, 14.0)$: a unit-load probe gives
peak von~Mises $119.8$~MPa, and the linearity of the elasticity operator
yields $w = 0.5\,\sigma_y / 119.8 = 0.8555$~MPa as the value that drives
the worst-case peak stress to exactly $0.5\,\sigma_y = 102.5$~MPa. The
same $w$ is used across all LHS samples, so the stress distribution
across the training set sweeps the full $\sim [50, 120]$~MPa elastic band
as $(R, p, W)$ vary.

\subsection{Finite-element discretization}

The problem is solved with FEniCSx~\cite{dokken_fenicsx} on unstructured
triangular meshes produced by Gmsh. Displacements are represented on a
Lagrange order-2 vector element (quadratic $T_6$ triangles), giving an
integration order sufficient to capture the quadratic stress distribution
through the flange thickness without mesh-doubling. Stresses are
recovered by differentiation and interpolated to a scalar Lagrange order-2
space; the von~Mises norm is formed pointwise as
$\sigma_{\mathrm{vM}} =
\sqrt{\sigma_{xx}^2 - \sigma_{xx}\sigma_{yy} + \sigma_{yy}^2
+ 3\sigma_{xy}^2}$, which is the correct plane-stress reduction of the
3D von~Mises norm.

Element-size fields localize refinement near the three stress
concentrators (inside fillet arc, Hole~1 perimeter, Hole~2 perimeter). A
distance-threshold field drops the characteristic length from
$h_{\text{fine}} = 0.2$~mm on these curves to $h_{\text{coarse}} = 2.0$~mm
on the far-field, transitioning over $8$~mm. The resulting meshes carry
$\sim 11{,}000$ scalar DOFs at the converged refinement level, with the
bulk of the nodal budget concentrated in the fillet region.

\subsection{LHS design and dataset generation}
\label{ssec:lhs}

Parameter triples are drawn with a seeded Latin Hypercube
design~\cite{psaros2023uq} over the joint box. The design oversamples to
absorb the geometric-feasibility rejection: $N_{\mathrm{draw}} = 5500$
candidate triples are generated, filtered by the constraint set described
in Section~\ref{ssec:bcs} and Figure~\ref{fig:geom}, and extended by
reseeded sub-draws if the accepted count falls short of the target
$N_{\mathrm{valid}} = 5000$. Each accepted triple is meshed, solved, and
packaged as a \texttt{PyTorch~Geometric} \texttt{Data} object carrying the
$T_3$ corner mesh (node coordinates, element connectivity), the full-order
Lagrange-2 displacement and von~Mises fields, and indicator sets marking
which nodes lie on each tagged boundary (clamped, loaded, fillet, hole~1,
hole~2). The in-distribution pool is partitioned 80/10/10 into training
($N_{\mathrm{train}} = 4000$), validation ($N_{\mathrm{val}} = 500$), and
test ($N_{\mathrm{test}} = 500$) sets by an LHS-stratified ordering over
the $(R, p, W)$ cube, and an additional $N_{\mathrm{ood}} = 250$ samples
are drawn under the pre-registered OOD protocol and held out of training,
validation, and test throughout.

\subsection{Pipeline validation}

Two independent consistency checks were run before the production sweep.

\textbf{Mesh convergence.} At the nominal sample
$(R, p, W) = (6.5, 57.0, 19.0)$, refinement was swept across four levels:
$(h_{\text{coarse}}, h_{\text{fine}}) =
(4.0, 1.5), (3.0, 0.8), (2.5, 0.4), (2.0, 0.2)$~mm. The peak von~Mises
changed by $+0.024\%$ between the two finest levels (Table~\ref{tab:conv}),
well below the $1\%$ target. The finest refinement
$(h_{\text{coarse}}, h_{\text{fine}}, d_{\text{refine}}) = (2.0, 0.2, 8.0)$
mm is adopted for the full sweep.

\textbf{Analytical cross-check.} An un-notched L (same outer outline, no
holes, sharp inside corner) is meshed at production refinement and solved
at unit load. At a cross-section $x = W + 15$~mm $= 34$~mm, the FEA
extreme-fibre stress is compared to the closed-form Euler--Bernoulli
cantilever prediction
$\sigma_b(x) = 3 w (B - x)^2 / W^2$. For $W = 19$~mm, $x = 34$~mm,
$w = 1$~MPa the analytical value is $17.58$~MPa; the FEA extreme-fibre
stress at the same section is $17.91$~MPa, a $+1.8\%$ deviation that is
within the expected residual from mesh-refinement bias near the sharp
corner (which is deliberately localized away from the probe section). The
pipeline is therefore trusted for the full sweep.

\subsection{Surrogate architecture}
\label{ssec:gnn}

The surrogate is a MeshGraphNet-style encoder--processor--decoder graph
neural network following Pfaff et al.~\cite{pfaff2021meshgraphnets}, adapted
to the stationary stress-prediction setting by Maurizi et
al.~\cite{maurizi2022gnn} (no temporal rollout; the decoder emits the
converged stress field in a single forward pass). Each FEA sample becomes
one graph $G = (V, E)$: $V$ is the set of $T_3$ corner nodes and $E$ is the
undirected edge list induced by the triangle connectivity (each triangle
contributes its three edges in both directions, $\sim 130$k edges per
batched graph at training time).

\textbf{Node features} ($13$ channels). Per-node: the 2D coordinate
$(x, y)$; a $5$-way one-hot over the tagged boundary classes
\{clamped, loaded, fillet, hole~1, hole~2\} plus a sixth indicator for
interior (free) nodes; the three graph-level design parameters
$(R, p, W)$ broadcast to every node so message passing carries global
conditioning information; and two geometric scalars --- the Euclidean
distance from each node to the nearest fillet node and to the nearest
hole-perimeter node. The one-hot node-type channel follows
MeshGraphNets' node-type
embedding~\cite{pfaff2021meshgraphnets}, which injects the boundary-condition
structure into the GNN's message passing without requiring the
aggregator to learn to infer type from coordinates alone.

\textbf{Edge features} ($4$ channels). Per directed edge: the relative
displacement $(\Delta x, \Delta y)$, the Euclidean length
$\lVert \Delta \rVert$, and the clipped inverse length
$1/\max(\lVert \Delta \rVert, 10^{-6})$, i.e.\ the same relative-position
encoding used by MeshGraphNets' mesh-edge features augmented with an
inverse-distance channel for sharper fillet-region locality.

\textbf{Encoder.} A node MLP $\varepsilon_V : \mathbb{R}^{13} \to
\mathbb{R}^H$ and an edge MLP $\varepsilon_M : \mathbb{R}^4 \to
\mathbb{R}^H$ lift the raw features to the processor's hidden width $H$.
Each MLP is two ReLU hidden layers wide-$H$ with a trailing LayerNorm.

\textbf{Processor.} $L$ identical message-passing blocks with residual
connections, matching the canonical MeshGraphNets processor
topology~\cite{pfaff2021meshgraphnets}. Block $\ell$ computes
\begin{align}
  \boldsymbol{e}_{ij}^{\ell+1}
  &= f^M_\ell\bigl(\boldsymbol{e}_{ij}^\ell,
                    \boldsymbol{n}_i^\ell,
                    \boldsymbol{n}_j^\ell\bigr)
   + \boldsymbol{e}_{ij}^\ell,
  \\
  \boldsymbol{n}_i^{\ell+1}
  &= f^V_\ell\Bigl(\boldsymbol{n}_i^\ell,
                    \sum_{j \in \mathcal{N}(i)}
                    \boldsymbol{e}_{ij}^{\ell+1}\Bigr)
   + \boldsymbol{n}_i^\ell,
\end{align}
with $f^M_\ell, f^V_\ell$ two-hidden-layer ReLU MLPs with LayerNorm. Sum
aggregation preserves permutation invariance of the incoming-neighbour
set.

\textbf{Decoder.} A node MLP $\delta : \mathbb{R}^H \to \mathbb{R}$ emits
per-node von~Mises stress in MPa, with no trailing LayerNorm so the output
scale is unconstrained.

Input features and edge attributes are normalized to zero mean and unit
variance using statistics estimated on the first $200$ training samples
and reused across all downstream runs (including every ensemble member);
targets remain in physical units so loss values and metrics report
directly in MPa.

\subsection{Training protocol}
\label{ssec:training}

Hyperparameters were selected by a lightweight sweep on the validation
split (Section~\ref{ssec:results-surrogate}) from the candidate grid
in~\cite{pfaff2021meshgraphnets}-style hidden width $H \in \{64, 128\}$,
processor depth $L \in \{3, 5\}$, and initial learning rate
$\alpha_0 \in \{5 \times 10^{-4}, 1 \times 10^{-3}\}$. The best
configuration, $H = 128$, $L = 5$, $\alpha_0 = 5 \times 10^{-4}$, matches
the default recommended by the MeshGraphNets processor ablations on
2D solid-mechanics benchmarks.

Training minimizes the Huber loss with $\delta = 1$~MPa, trading the
gradient sensitivity of mean-squared error for robustness to the long
right tail of the von~Mises stress distribution that concentrates around
the fillet and hole rims. The Adam optimizer~\cite{pfaff2021meshgraphnets}
is stepped with initial learning rate $\alpha_0 = 5 \times 10^{-4}$ and
cosine annealed to $10^{-5}$ over the full budget. Training runs for up
to $60$ epochs at batch size $8$, with early stopping on validation loss
with patience $12$ epochs; in practice every member converges well before
the cap. All training is performed locally on a single NVIDIA RTX~4060
Laptop GPU (8~GB VRAM) in a 24~GB system-RAM Windows environment.

\subsection{Deep Ensembles for epistemic uncertainty}
\label{ssec:ensembles}

Following Lakshminarayanan et al.~\cite{lakshminarayanan2017ensembles}, we
train a Deep Ensemble of $M = 5$ independently initialized GNN models. Each
member uses the same architecture and the same training data, differing
only in (i) the random seed controlling weight initialization and (ii) the
dataloader-shuffle stream, so that member diversity arises purely from
initialization plus SGD trajectory. The ensemble size $M = 5$ is the
value identified by Lakshminarayanan et al.\ as capturing most of the
variance benefit of ensembling, with sharply diminishing returns beyond.

At inference, the member predictions
$\{\hat{\sigma}_m(\mathbf{x}_i)\}_{m=1}^M$ at each node are combined into a
point estimate $\bar{\sigma}(\mathbf{x}_i) = (1/M) \sum_m \hat{\sigma}_m$
and an epistemic-uncertainty scalar
$s(\mathbf{x}_i) = \mathrm{std}_m \hat{\sigma}_m(\mathbf{x}_i)$, where the
standard deviation is taken across members. Under the interpretation
motivated in Lakshminarayanan et al.\ and discussed in the Psaros UQ
survey~\cite{psaros2023uq}, member disagreement is a proxy for epistemic
uncertainty: the model does not know what it does not know, so disagreement
between equally-plausible fits of the same training distribution is
expected to rise in regions where the training signal is sparse or the
mapping is poorly constrained. The calibration quality of this proxy --- the
empirical correlation between $s(\mathbf{x}_i)$ and the actual error
$\lvert \bar{\sigma}(\mathbf{x}_i) - \sigma_\star(\mathbf{x}_i)\rvert$ ---
is the raw input to the Phase-2 CQR layer and is evaluated directly in
Section~\ref{ssec:results-calibration}.

\subsection{Conformalized Quantile Regression}
\label{ssec:cqr}

The Phase-1 ensemble produces, at every mesh node, a predictive mean
$\bar{\sigma}(\mathbf{x}_i)$ and a standard deviation
$s(\mathbf{x}_i)$ across the $M = 5$ members. We turn these into a pair of
base quantile predictors via a Gaussian ansatz,
\begin{equation}
  \hat{q}_{\text{lo}}(\mathbf{x}_i) = \bar{\sigma}(\mathbf{x}_i)
    - z_{1-\alpha/2}\,s(\mathbf{x}_i),
  \qquad
  \hat{q}_{\text{hi}}(\mathbf{x}_i) = \bar{\sigma}(\mathbf{x}_i)
    + z_{1-\alpha/2}\,s(\mathbf{x}_i),
\end{equation}
with $z_{1-\alpha/2} = \Phi^{-1}(1-\alpha/2)$ the standard-normal quantile
and $\alpha$ the target miscoverage. No auxiliary quantile network is
trained: the Phase-1 result that ensemble disagreement tracks error
(Section~\ref{ssec:results-calibration}) is the empirical justification for
taking $s(\mathbf{x}_i)$ as the scale on which to construct quantile
bounds. Using ensemble std as a conformal nonconformity input is one of the
three base-score choices surveyed in Gopakumar et
al.~\cite{gopakumar2024conformal}.

Following Romano et al.~\cite{romano2019cqr} (Algorithm~1, Equations~9--11),
we apply split-conformal calibration. On the calibration set (see
Section~\ref{ssec:cqr-calib} for the set choice) we compute the CQR
nonconformity score at every node,
\begin{equation}
  E_{ij} = \max\!\bigl(
    \hat{q}_{\text{lo}}(\mathbf{x}_i^{(j)}) - \sigma^\star(\mathbf{x}_i^{(j)}),\;
    \sigma^\star(\mathbf{x}_i^{(j)}) - \hat{q}_{\text{hi}}(\mathbf{x}_i^{(j)})
  \bigr),
\end{equation}
indexed over calibration sample $j$ and mesh node $i$. A positive score
means the true stress fell outside the base interval; a negative score
means the base interval was wider than needed (slack). The conformal
quantile correction is
\begin{equation}
  \hat{Q}(\alpha) = \operatorname{Quantile}_{\lceil (n+1)(1-\alpha)\rceil / n}
    \bigl( \{E_{ij}\} \bigr),
  \label{eq:qhat}
\end{equation}
where $n$ is the total number of calibration $(i,j)$ pairs and the ceiling
is the standard finite-sample correction. The final CQR-calibrated
prediction interval is
\begin{equation}
  \hat{C}_\alpha(\mathbf{x}) = \bigl[
    \hat{q}_{\text{lo}}(\mathbf{x}) - \hat{Q}(\alpha),\;
    \hat{q}_{\text{hi}}(\mathbf{x}) + \hat{Q}(\alpha)
  \bigr].
  \label{eq:cqr-interval}
\end{equation}
Romano et al.\ Theorem~1 gives the distribution-free finite-sample
guarantee
$\Pr\{\sigma^\star(\mathbf{x}) \in \hat{C}_\alpha(\mathbf{x})\} \geq 1-\alpha$
under exchangeability of the calibration--test nodes. Because pooling scores
across nodes within the same sample is only a marginal guarantee --- nodes
in one graph are not independent --- coverage is reported at the marginal
node level across the test set, with conditional coverage probed
empirically by slicing on design-parameter quartiles.

\subsubsection{Calibration set choice and validation-reuse disclosure}
\label{ssec:cqr-calib}

CQR requires an exchangeable calibration set held out from training. We use
the $N_{\mathrm{val}} = 500$ in-distribution validation samples as the
calibration set. This set was previously used in Phase~1 for a restricted
hyperparameter selection (ranking three candidate
$(H, L, \alpha_0)$ configurations on val per-node MAPE; see
Section~\ref{ssec:results-surrogate}). The selection therefore induces a
minor optimistic bias in the CQR empirical coverage reported on the test
set, because the ensemble point predictor was tuned once on the same
samples whose residuals now seed the conformal quantile $\hat{Q}$. The
adaptation is bounded: only three configurations were evaluated, only the
point-accuracy metric was inspected, and the selected configuration
happens to match the default from~\cite{pfaff2021meshgraphnets} (so the
choice is not strongly data-adaptive). We disclose this reuse rather than
mask it with a second held-out split, both because the sample budget does
not support a four-way partition and because conservative coverage
margins (Section~\ref{ssec:results-cqr}) comfortably absorb the effect.

\subsection{Comparison with the uncalibrated ensemble interval}

For every nominal level $1-\alpha$ we also report the raw Gaussian ensemble
interval $\bar{\sigma} \pm z_{1-\alpha/2} s$ without the conformal
correction. This is the ``deep-ensemble prediction interval'' an engineer
would naively read off the ensemble output. The comparison isolates what
the CQR layer adds: a distribution-free coverage guarantee, as distinct
from an in-distribution calibration assumption that the member distribution
is approximately Gaussian.

\subsection{Out-of-distribution evaluation protocol}
\label{ssec:ood-protocol}

Conformal prediction's coverage guarantee (Equation~\ref{eq:cqr-interval}
and Theorem~1 of~\cite{romano2019cqr}) holds under exchangeability of the
calibration and test sets. Out-of-distribution (OOD) inputs --- geometries
drawn from parameter values the surrogate did not see during training ---
violate exchangeability by construction, so neither the point predictor
nor the CQR coverage carry any formal guarantee in that regime. The
deployment-relevant question is empirical: \emph{how much does the
reliability layer degrade on OOD inputs, and does the degradation carry a
usable signal for the engineer} (a wider interval or a larger ensemble
std) \emph{or does the surrogate fail silently}?

\textbf{Pre-registration.} To make the OOD evaluation falsifiable, the
extrapolation protocol was fixed in writing before any model was trained
(see~\texttt{paper/NOTES.md} Day-3 entry): the axes, magnitudes, and sample
allocation are specified below and were not revised in light of Phase-1 or
Phase-2 results. This structurally rules out post-hoc cherry-picking of
extrapolation directions to match an observed failure mode.

\textbf{Extrapolation axes and magnitudes.} Each of the three design
parameters $(R, p, W)$ is extended $\pm 20\%$ of its training span beyond
the training bound, clipped where the geometric-feasibility constraints
(minimum fillet radius, bolt-hole clearance, Hole-2 tip clearance) bind.
The $\pm 20\%$ setting is the standard surrogate-paper extrapolation
magnitude: clearly outside training while leaving a meaningful feasible
volume after rejection.

\textbf{Sample allocation.} $N_{\mathrm{ood}} = 250$ samples in total,
partitioned into seven conditions:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Single-parameter OOD} (150 samples, 25 per direction).
    One of $R, p, W$ drawn from its OOD sub-interval while the other two
    are drawn from their training ranges. Six directions: $R$-low, $R$-high,
    $p$-low, $p$-high, $W$-low, $W$-high.
  \item \textbf{Corner OOD} (100 samples). Two or three parameters
    simultaneously out of their training ranges, drawn by rejection-filtered
    Latin Hypercube on the expanded box.
\end{itemize}
The single-parameter split probes clean attribution of surrogate error to
individual parameter axes; the corner split probes the joint-extrapolation
regime where failure modes typically compound. Sample seeds are pinned in
the OOD dataset manifest, allowing the exact set to be reconstructed.

\textbf{Reported metrics.} All Phase-1 and Phase-2 metrics (per-node MAPE,
peak-stress MAPE, CQR empirical coverage at nominal $90\%$, mean CQR
interval width, mean ensemble std) are re-computed on OOD and reported
side-by-side with the ID test metrics. No post-hoc exclusion of ``outlier''
samples is permitted.

\subsection{Deferral decision rule}
\label{ssec:deferral}

For deployment we convert the ensemble disagreement signal --- introduced
as an out-of-distribution indicator by Lakshminarayanan et
al.~\cite{lakshminarayanan2017ensembles} and reaffirmed as a practical
epistemic-uncertainty proxy in the surrogate UQ
survey~\cite{psaros2023uq} --- into a concrete
binary decision rule: \emph{use the surrogate's prediction} when the
per-sample mean ensemble standard deviation $\bar{s}(\mathbf{x})$ is below
a threshold $T$, and \emph{defer to FEA} otherwise. The threshold is
calibrated from the in-distribution test distribution: $T^\star$ is the
$95^{\mathrm{th}}$ percentile of $\bar{s}$ on the 500-sample ID test set,
so that the false-alarm rate on ID inputs is $5\%$ by construction. We
evaluate $T^\star$ on the 250-sample OOD set to report the detection rate
(fraction of OOD samples correctly flagged for deferral), and we sweep $T$
across a range of ID quantiles to trace the full operating characteristic.

\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

Data is split as described in Section~\ref{ssec:lhs}: $N_{\mathrm{train}}
= 4000$, $N_{\mathrm{val}} = 500$, $N_{\mathrm{test}} = 500$, all drawn by
LHS-stratified ordering from the 5000-sample main pool, with an additional
$N_{\mathrm{ood}} = 250$ held out under the pre-registered OOD protocol
and not touched in Phase~1. All hyperparameter decisions are made on the
validation split; the test split is used only once for the final
evaluation reported below.

We report three accuracy metrics, computed on the in-distribution test
split:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Per-node MAPE} ---
    $\mathrm{MAPE} = \mathrm{mean}_{i,k}
    \lvert \hat{\sigma}_{ik} - \sigma^\star_{ik} \rvert /
    \max(\lvert \sigma^\star_{ik} \rvert, \varepsilon)$
    over all nodes $i$ and all test samples $k$, with
    $\varepsilon = 1$~MPa preventing near-zero stresses in unloaded
    regions from inflating the percent error.
  \item \textbf{Peak-stress MAPE} --- the same relative error computed on
    the per-sample maximum von~Mises stress, which is the
    deployment-relevant scalar an engineer reads off the bracket.
  \item \textbf{Absolute error percentiles} --- the 50th, 90th, 99th
    and max of $\lvert \hat{\sigma}_{ik} - \sigma^\star_{ik} \rvert$ in
    MPa, to expose tail behaviour that MAPE's mean compresses. This
    follows the evaluation practice of Maurizi et
    al.~\cite{maurizi2022gnn} and the RMSE-based tabulation in
    MeshGraphNets~\cite{pfaff2021meshgraphnets}.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Ensemble calibration}
\label{ssec:results-calibration}

The central Phase-1 claim is that ensemble disagreement tracks actual
prediction error --- a necessary property for the Phase-2 CQR layer to
operate on a meaningful raw uncertainty signal. We quantify this at two
granularities.

\textbf{Node-level.} Across all $\sim 1.4{\times}10^6$ test-set nodes, we
compute the ensemble standard deviation $s(\mathbf{x}_i)$ and the absolute
residual $r(\mathbf{x}_i) = \lvert \bar{\sigma}(\mathbf{x}_i) -

gives a per-sample scatter (Figure~\ref{fig:phase1-calib}, right panel).
This is the deployment-relevant view: a design engineer asks ``for this
candidate bracket, can I trust the surrogate?'' and the ensemble answers
with a sample-level uncertainty. The positive slope is the empirical
foundation for the Phase-2 CQR prediction interval.

\label{ssec:results-cqr}

split. Table~\ref{tab:phase2-coverage} reports empirical coverage and mean

At every nominal level the CQR interval attains at or above the target
coverage (82.7\%, 87.0\%, 91.3\%, 95.5\% against nominals of 80\%, 85\%,

produces a loud, measurable, actionable signal that the ambient
uncertainty is larger.

\subsection{Deployment recommendation: defer-to-FEA decision rule}
\label{ssec:results-deferral}

The final deliverable of the reliability layer is a concrete, deployable
decision rule. Following Section~\ref{ssec:deferral}, we threshold the
per-sample mean ensemble std at $T^\star = 0.182$~MPa, the
$95^{\mathrm{th}}$ percentile of the ID test distribution. A sample above
the threshold is flagged as ``defer to FEA''; a sample below is trusted
to the surrogate. Table~\ref{tab:phase3-deferral} reports the operating
characteristic at four candidate threshold quantiles, and
Figure~\ref{fig:phase3-deferral-roc} plots the full ROC curve together
with the ID--OOD std histograms.

At the $95^{\mathrm{th}}$ percentile operating point, the rule flags
$5\%$ of ID samples unnecessarily (the target false-alarm rate by
construction) and correctly flags $34\%$ of the full OOD set. The
per-condition detection rates are informative: $0\%$ for both $R$-low and
$R$-high (the surrogate extrapolates cleanly on the fillet axis and the
reliability layer does not disagree), $4$--$44\%$ on the remaining
single-parameter conditions, and $58\%$ on the corner-OOD samples --- the
regime where extrapolation is jointly hardest and where the deferral rule
is most useful. The AUROC of the per-sample std as an OOD detector is
$0.673$: moderate, as expected given that some OOD conditions ($R$) are
in practice easier for the surrogate than the hardest quartile of ID
samples, so no scalar score can perfectly separate the two populations.
For deployment this is the correct tradeoff: we would rather defer a
difficult ID sample to FEA than trust a badly extrapolated corner-OOD
geometry.

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/fig_phase3_deferral_roc.pdf}
  \caption{Phase-3 defer-to-FEA decision rule. \emph{Left:} ROC of the
    per-sample mean ensemble std as an OOD detector; the marker shows
    the deployment operating point at $T^\star =$ ID-std $95^{\mathrm{th}}$

Practically, the rule is a drop-in safety layer on top of the surrogate:
given a new $(R, p, W)$ triple, run the 5 ensemble members and compute
$\bar{s}$. If $\bar{s} > T^\star$, recommend a full FEA run; otherwise
return the CQR-calibrated stress field with its guaranteed coverage
interval. The compute overhead of the deferral check is negligible (5
forward passes plus a scalar comparison); the trust overhead is a single
tunable parameter --- the ID-std quantile at which to place the
threshold --- that the design team can choose based on how costly a false
alarm (unnecessary FEA) is relative to a missed OOD (silent
extrapolation).

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{What CQR adds over raw ensembles.}
Phase~1 established a strong empirical correlation between ensemble
disagreement and prediction error, but disagreement alone is a scalar
without a probabilistic interpretation: two ensembles can share the same
Pearson correlation with the truth while producing arbitrarily
mis-calibrated intervals. Phase~2 converts that correlated-but-uncalibrated
signal into a prediction interval with a distribution-free finite-sample
coverage guarantee (Equation~\ref{eq:cqr-interval}, Theorem~1
of~\cite{romano2019cqr}). For the L-bracket surrogate specifically, the
raw Gaussian ensemble interval is already close to calibrated, and the
conformal correction $\hat{Q}$ remains sub-MPa at every tested level ---
the surrogate is a friendly case where CQR's marginal contribution is
modest in magnitude but categorical in interpretation.

\paragraph{Practical implications for deployment.}
A design engineer consuming the surrogate receives, at any point in the
allowed parameter cube, a full per-node stress field bracketed by a
$1-\alpha$ prediction interval whose width is data-adaptive (narrow in
the bulk, wide at stress concentrators) and whose coverage is guaranteed
on in-distribution inputs by conformal theory and verified on the
held-out test set. The engineer can pick $\alpha$ to match the safety
margin required by the downstream decision --- $\alpha = 0.05$ for a
conservative sizing check, $\alpha = 0.10$ or $0.20$ for rapid
exploration --- without re-training or re-calibrating.

\paragraph{OOD: the reliability layer as a flag, not a fix.}
The Phase-3 results make the scope of the reliability layer precise. On
in-distribution inputs, the CQR wrapper delivers on the Theorem~1
guarantee; on OOD inputs, the guarantee expires, but the ensemble-std
signal does not. The reliability layer therefore plays two distinct roles:
a \emph{coverage-guaranteeing interval} inside the training envelope, and
an \emph{OOD detector} at its boundary. The defer-to-FEA decision rule
(Section~\ref{ssec:results-deferral}) operationalizes the second role: it
does not try to fix OOD predictions, it flags them and returns control to
the reference solver. This is the correct delegation pattern for a
neural-surrogate deployment: inside a documented envelope the surrogate
buys speed at a documented risk; outside, the surrogate admits it is out
of its depth and the FEA solver takes over.

\paragraph{Which extrapolation axes are safe?}
The per-condition breakdown (Table~\ref{tab:phase3-conditional}) carries
a concrete engineering message: on this geometry, the surrogate
extrapolates cleanly on the fillet radius $R$ in both directions but not
on the hole position $p$ or the flange width $W$, and not at all on
joint-parameter extrapolation. For a design team considering a $\pm 20\%$
widening of their search box, this decomposition directly informs which
axes can be widened with confidence and which require fresh FEA coverage.
Any similar surrogate rollout should publish this per-axis extrapolation
audit alongside the headline accuracy number.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
(i) Coverage is marginal, not conditional: conditional coverage is
verified empirically by quartile slicing but is not theoretically
guaranteed; covariate-shift extensions of conformal
prediction~\cite{angelopoulos2023conformal} would formalize the OOD case
and are a natural follow-up.
(ii) The calibration set doubles as the Phase-1 hyperparameter-selection
set (Section~\ref{ssec:cqr-calib}); the adaptation is documented and
bounded but not zero.
(iii) Pooled node-level calibration treats mesh nodes within a graph as
exchangeable, which they are not in a strict sense (nodes share a
coherent underlying stress field). This is the standard
cell-wise-pooling assumption from~\cite{gopakumar2024conformal}; the
observation that sample-level correlation ($0.944$) is far higher than
node-level correlation ($0.494$) in Phase~1 suggests the relevant
signal lives at the sample aggregation level.
(iv) No aleatoric uncertainty layer: the FEA ground truth is deterministic
and noise-free, so all variance in the reliability layer is epistemic by
construction; extending to noisy measurements would require a
heteroscedastic quantile head rather than the Gaussian ensemble ansatz.
(v) The physical model is idealized: 2D plane stress (justified by the
$t/A = 1/10$ thin-plate aspect ratio and documented in
Section~\ref{sec:experiments}), a single homogeneous isotropic material
(AISI~304, representative of a thin shelf bracket), linear elasticity
only (the load is calibrated so peak stress stays at $\sim 50\%$ of
$\sigma_y$), and self-weight omitted (estimated at $\sim 10^{-3}$ of the
load-driven peak). Relaxing any of these takes the reliability layer
through a new calibration set --- the scaffold transfers, the numbers do
not. (vi) The OOD protocol covers $\pm 20\%$ single-axis and
$\geq 2$-axis extrapolation; larger extrapolation magnitudes, as well as
\emph{structural} extrapolation (changing the number of holes, adding a
second fillet, moving to asymmetric arms) are outside the scope of this
evaluation.

\paragraph{Future work.}
The geometry set studied here is deliberately minimal; the natural next
steps are (a) asymmetric-arm L-brackets and more general bracket
topologies, where the $(R, p, W)$ parameterization generalizes to a
higher-dimensional design vector; (b) multiple independent load cases
(off-axis shelf loads, shock loads) per geometry, turning the decoder
into a parametric conditional map; (c) nonlinear material response
(plasticity, hyperelasticity) which breaks the linear-load-scaling short-cut
used in Section~\ref{ssec:bcs} and requires the conformal layer to be
re-calibrated per load level; and (d) extension to 3D, where plane-stress
reduction is no longer available and the GNN input carries the full
volumetric mesh --- here the mesh-based architecture transfers directly
but the per-evaluation compute budget rises. A covariate-shift-aware
conformal extension~\cite{angelopoulos2023conformal} would also be
valuable: rather than a binary defer-to-FEA rule, a continuous
re-calibration of $\hat{Q}$ as a function of an estimated
density-ratio score.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We have built a deployment-calibrated neural surrogate for parametric
L-bracket stress prediction by stacking a MeshGraphNets-style GNN, a
5-member Deep Ensemble, and a split-conformalized quantile-regression
layer on a common FEA training set. The surrogate attains $1.80\%$
per-node and $0.42\%$ peak-stress MAPE on a 500-sample in-distribution
test set; the conformal layer delivers the Romano~2019 Theorem~1
coverage guarantee empirically at four nominal levels
($1 - \alpha \in \{0.80, 0.85, 0.90, 0.95\}$); and the interval width is
informative, correlating with the surrogate's actual error at
Pearson~$0.944$ per sample.

On a 250-sample pre-registered OOD test set, the same frozen pipeline
shows the expected behaviour: accuracy degrades
($3.10\%$ per-node and $7.26\%$ peak MAPE), empirical coverage falls
below nominal ($83.7\%$ against $90\%$), and --- the point of the paper
--- the reliability layer's own uncertainty signal grows to match, with
CQR interval width and ensemble std both roughly doubling. The worst
regime is corner OOD (two or more parameters simultaneously outside the
training box), where the ensemble std reaches $3.5\times$ the ID baseline.
We convert the std signal into a concrete defer-to-FEA decision rule
calibrated at $5\%$ false-alarm rate on ID inputs, which detects $58\%$
of corner-OOD samples and $34\%$ of the overall OOD set.

The practical takeaway is that a conformalized Deep Ensemble on top of a
GNN surrogate is sufficient to equip a parametric FEA replacement with
\emph{both} a coverage guarantee inside the training envelope and a
usable OOD flag at its boundary --- without training any additional
network or touching the base surrogate. The reliability layer is a retrofit,
not a redesign, and all of its machinery is already standard in
contemporary uncertainty-quantification and conformal-prediction
literature. The contribution is the combination and the honest evaluation
under a pre-registered protocol.
